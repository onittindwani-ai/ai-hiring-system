# 12. Law, fairness and privacy

## Purpose
A plain overview of the rules that most often touch hiring, so you can ask a lawyer sharper questions.
**This is not legal advice.** Laws change, differ by place and by company size, and apply to facts. Every dated claim below says how it was checked.

## How to read the checks
- "checked on 2026-11-20, verify before relying on it" means: looked up on that date.
- "primary page read" means the official source was opened and read that day.
- "secondary only" means the official page could not be opened or read that day, and the claim comes from law firm or news summaries. Treat it as a lead, not a fact.
- If a date or status could not be verified, the doc says so. Do not fill the gap with a guess.

## Do this
1. Say where you hire: the country, state or city of the job, and where the candidates live. Law follows both. Ask the owner (below) if you do not know.
2. Use job related criteria only. Write them in the scorecard before screening. This is the single best protection, in any country.
3. Keep the hiring decision with a person. Tools rank and draft. Every rejection and offer is a human decision with a written reason.
4. Give notice where the law may require it: tell candidates when automated tools help screen them, and how to ask for a person or an accommodation. It is also good practice everywhere.
5. Collect the minimum data, use it only for hiring, keep it for a set time, and delete it after (see data protection).
6. Keep records: the post, the scorecard, scores and notes, the messages, the decisions and the reasons, for the time local law requires (see record keeping).
7. Test your process for unfair results: compare how groups move through the funnel, only where you lawfully hold that data, and ask a professional how (see the audit idea below).
8. Before you hire across borders or use a contractor, ask a local expert (doc 14).
9. Before you launch a new tool in a place with an AI hiring rule, ask a lawyer whether it applies to you.

## Fairness basics that hold almost everywhere
- Do not decide on protected traits (the exact list is local): common ones are race, colour, sex or gender, pregnancy, religion, national origin, age, disability, genetic information, and in many places family status, sexual orientation or caste. Never use a close proxy (a name, a photo, a zip code, graduation year as an age guess, an accent).
- Disparate impact: a neutral rule can still be unlawful if it screens out a protected group and is not job related. So every criterion needs a job related reason.
- Accommodations: in many places you must make reasonable adjustments in hiring for disability, and sometimes religion. Invite people to ask, and respond.
- Do not ask questions that reveal protected traits (doc 10 list).
- Treat every candidate the same way in the same stage.
- Retaliation against a person who raises a concern is unlawful in many places. Take concerns seriously.

## United States (overview)
The US has federal, state and city rules. Which ones apply depends on where the job is and on company size.

### Federal anti discrimination basics
- Title VII (race, colour, religion, sex, national origin), the ADA (disability) and the ADEA (age 40 and over) cover hiring for employers above set size thresholds. The EEOC enforces them. Primary source: eeoc.gov. Stable, long standing statutes; checked on 2026-11-20, verify before relying on it (the eeoc.gov guidance pages could not be opened that day, so the thresholds and details are not verified here).
- The "four fifths" rule of thumb (selection rate of a group below 80 percent of the highest group may signal adverse impact) comes from the federal Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures (29 CFR 1607). A trade press report said the EEOC was moving to partly rescind those guidelines; the final status was not confirmed. Secondary only. Use the ratio as a warning light, not a legal test, and ask a lawyer.
- AI in hiring: EEOC technical guidance documents on AI and selection tools were reported removed from its website in late January 2025 and not replaced (secondary only, checked on 2026-11-20, verify before relying on it). The underlying laws still apply to AI assisted decisions. Do not read the removal as permission.

### New York City, Local Law 144 (automated employment decision tools)
- What it requires, per the NYC consumer and worker agency page (primary page read, checked on 2026-11-20, verify before relying on it): an automated employment decision tool used for hiring or promotion decisions must have had a bias audit within one year before use; information about the audit must be public (a summary of results); and candidates and employees must get notice, which the agency says must be given 10 business days before use.
- Enforcement by the agency began on 5 July 2023 (primary page read).
- A New York State Comptroller audit in December 2025 reviewed the city's enforcement (secondary summaries; not read in full).
- It applies to jobs in New York City. Whether a given tool counts as an "AEDT" depends on whether it substantially assists or replaces discretionary decisions. Ask a lawyer.
- Primary source: nyc.gov (the AEDT page of the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection).

### Illinois, HB 3773 (artificial intelligence in employment)
- Reported to amend the Illinois Human Rights Act, signed 9 August 2024 and effective 1 January 2026. It bars the use of AI that has a discriminatory effect in recruitment, hiring and other employment decisions, bars using zip codes as a proxy for protected classes, and requires notice to employees when AI is used in covered decisions. It does not require a formal bias audit (secondary only: the official ilga.gov page could not be opened on 2026-11-20; checked on 2026-11-20, verify before relying on it).
- Rules for the notice are for the state human rights department to set. Not verified whether those rules are final.

### Colorado
- The Colorado AI Act (SB 24-205) was the first broad state law on high risk AI decisions, including employment. It was delayed to 30 June 2026 in August 2025 (secondary summaries).
- Law firm summaries from mid 2026 report that Colorado repealed and replaced it with a narrower law (SB 26-189, an "automated decision making technology" act) signed 14 May 2026, effective 1 January 2027, with notice and consumer rights, no private right of action, and a federal court stay in April 2026 (secondary only).
- The legislature's own page for SB 24-205 (leg.colorado.gov), when read on 2026-11-20, still showed the 2024 text and listed no later bill or delay. That conflicts with the law firm reports, and this overview cannot tell which is current. Treat the status as unverified: ask a Colorado lawyer or check the legislature's page for the newest bill.

### California
- The Civil Rights Council approved employment regulations on automated decision systems on 27 June 2025, effective 1 October 2025 (primary page read on calcivilrights.ca.gov, checked on 2026-11-20, verify before relying on it).
- Per that page: employers must keep job related records, including data from automated decision systems, for at least four years; an automated decision system can violate the law if it harms applicants or employees on a protected basis; and automated tests, questions or games that reveal a disability may count as an unlawful medical inquiry.
- Other California bills on automated decisions at work were reported in 2025; status not verified.
- Primary source: calcivilrights.ca.gov.

### Other states and cities
Several other states and cities have passed or proposed rules on automated hiring tools, video interview analysis, or biometric data. The list changes often and was not verified here. Ask your lawyer to check the places where you hire.

## European Union (overview)
- **EU AI Act.** The AI Act treats AI systems used in recruitment and selection (placing targeted job ads, analysing and filtering applications, evaluating candidates) as "high risk". High risk duties include risk management, data quality, human oversight, logs, and information to the people affected. This reading of Annex III point 4 is from general knowledge; the primary EUR-Lex page could not be loaded that day, so verify on eur-lex.europa.eu.
- **Application date.** The original date for the high risk duties was 2 August 2026. Law firm summaries report that a 2026 "digital omnibus on AI" regulation (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026) moved the date for stand alone high risk systems, including employment, to 2 December 2027 (secondary only, checked on 2026-11-20, verify before relying on it; confirm the date and the regulation number in the Official Journal on eur-lex.europa.eu).
- **Banned practices.** Some uses are banned outright, reported to include emotion recognition in the workplace, in place since 2025 (general knowledge, verify).
- **GDPR.** Applies when you handle personal data of people in the EU, whatever your location. Key ideas: a lawful basis, fairness and transparency, data minimisation, storage limits, security, and rights to access, correct and delete. Article 22 limits decisions based solely on automated processing that have a significant effect on a person, so keep a human in the decision (general knowledge, verify).
- **Pay transparency directive** (Directive (EU) 2023/970): Article 5 gives job applicants the right to pay information before the interview (a starting pay or range) and bars asking about past pay (primary page on eur-lex.europa.eu read, in summary, on 2026-11-20). The transposition deadline is reported as 7 June 2026, and most member states were reported to have missed it (secondary only). National laws now differ by country.
- Each country adds its own labour law. Ask a local expert.

## Data protection basics (GDPR style, useful anywhere)
| Idea | In plain words | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lawful basis | you need a reason to hold the data | hiring is a common basis; say what you use it for |
| Minimum data | only what you need | no photos, birth dates or ID numbers in screening |
| Purpose | only for hiring | do not add candidates to marketing lists |
| Transparency | tell people | a short privacy note on the application form |
| Retention | do not keep forever | set a period, delete after (see numbers) |
| Access | people can ask what you hold | keep files findable (`hiring/<role>/`) |
| Security | keep it safe | local files, access limited, no candidate data in chats you do not control |
| Outside services | know where data goes | send only redacted text to any outside tool; tell candidates |
Jev or any outside model: if you use one, candidate text leaves your computer. Redact first (`tools/redact.py`), say so in your privacy note, and read the provider's own terms (doc 08 for what is known on Jev).

## Record keeping
- Keep for each role: the post, the scorecard, how and when it was published, the applicants' materials, scores and notes, interview ratings, messages, decisions, reasons, and the offer.
- Minimum retention periods are set locally. California states four years for job related records including automated decision data (primary page, above). Other places differ; ask a lawyer. Do not guess.
- Keep it in `hiring/<role>/`. That folder is git ignored so it is not published by mistake.
- When the period ends, delete or anonymise. Keep a short note of what you deleted and when.
- If a dispute starts, stop deleting and ask a lawyer.

## Pay transparency (trend, verify)
- A growing number of places require pay ranges in job posts or on request, and some ban asking for pay history. The EU directive above is one. Some US states and cities have similar rules, and the list changes often (not verified here).
- Good practice anywhere: put an honest range in every post. It builds trust and saves time (doc 07).
- `tools/bias_check.py` warns when a job post has no pay range.

## The AI audit idea (simple, not a legal audit)
Where the law requires a formal independent bias audit (NYC), hire an auditor. For everyone else, a simple check helps:
1. Lawfully collect optional, voluntary demographic data in a separate file, never visible to scorers, if your local law allows it.
2. After a batch, compare the pass rate by group at each stage.
3. If one group passes at much lower rates (for example under four fifths of the top group, as a warning light), look for the cause: a criterion, a keyword, the work sample, an interviewer.
4. Fix and note it. Ask a lawyer before acting on group data.
Never use that data inside scoring.

## Common mistakes
- Assuming "the AI decided" is a defence. You are responsible for your hiring decisions.
- Using a tool that screens people out without a human check.
- Collecting more data than needed (photos, ID numbers, health).
- Asking pay history where banned.
- Hiring across borders without checking the worker classification rules.
- Keeping candidate files forever.
- Treating this doc as legal advice.
- Copying a rule from a blog without checking the date.

## Numbers
- Retention: pick a period and write it in your privacy note. A common starting assumption is 6 to 12 months for unsuccessful candidates, replace with your local legal minimum.
- Four fifths ratio: 0.8 (a rule of thumb, not a legal test).
- Notice lead time in NYC: 10 business days (primary page read, 2026-11-20).
- Record minimum in California: 4 years (primary page read, 2026-11-20).

## Questions for a local lawyer
1. Which anti discrimination and AI or automated decision rules apply to our roles, places and size?
2. Do we need a bias audit, a notice, or a candidate right to a human, and how do we word the notice?
3. What may we ask in interviews and in the application form where the job is?
4. What data may we collect and how long must or may we keep it? What do we tell candidates?
5. Employee, contractor or employer of record for this person, and what are the risks of getting it wrong?
6. What may we check in references and background checks, and what consent is needed?
7. What must a job post show (pay, language, other) in this place?

## Ask the owner
1. In which country, state and city is the job, and where will candidates live? Default: assume the owner's own city and ask once.
2. About how many employees does the company have? Default: under 50.
3. Do you use any outside tool that sees candidate text, including Jev or a form tool? Default: Claude only, plus the owner's form and sheet.
4. Do you have a privacy note for applicants? Default: Claude drafts a short one for you to approve.
5. Is there a lawyer or adviser you already use? Default: none, so keep the question list above for one paid hour.
